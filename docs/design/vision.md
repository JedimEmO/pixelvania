# Vision

A pixel art metroidvania about nihilistic hedonism:
if nothing means anything, what is left is how things feel,
and the game asks whether that is enough.

This page holds the theme, the feelings we want the player to have,
and the world and content that carry them.
Every other design page answers to this one.

## The idea in one paragraph

The world is a party that never ended.
A palace of feasts, gardens, ballrooms and baths,
where the guests kept going long after anyone remembered why.
You are one of them, waking up late and alone, chasing the sound of the party through the rooms.
The game never says the chase is pointless, and it never says it is not.
It makes the chase feel wonderful, then lets you notice what the wonder costs.

## Pillars

Movement is the pleasure.
The most honest thing in the game is how it feels to run, jump and fall.
If moving through an empty room is not a joy on its own, nothing else will save the game.
This is why game feel is a pillar and not a polish pass.

Every blow has weight.
Exploring is light and fast; fighting is heavy and committed.
The contrast is the point, and [combat](combat.md) is built around it.

Pleasure has a price, and the price is quiet.
Every high in the game is followed by a comedown.
We never punish indulgence with a game over or a lecture;
we let the world get a little greyer, a little quieter, a little slower.

Light is where the colour lives.
The world's surfaces are dark and tired, and only light is vivid.
Pleasures glow; everything else is a silhouette waiting to be lit.
This is the art rule, and it is also the theme drawn as a picture.

The game does not preach.
It explores the idea from inside and leaves the answer to the player.
No character is right, and no ending is the good one.

## What the player should feel

The feelings follow the arc of a long night.
Each part of the world is built around one of them,
and art, music and mechanics in that area all push in the same direction.

| Stage of the night | Feeling | How we deliver it |
|---|---|---|
| Arrival | Anticipation, seduction | Warm candlelight, distant music through walls, doors that promise more than they show |
| The feast | Abundance, then too much | Dense rooms, rich reds and gold, food that rots at the edges of the screen |
| The gardens | Sensual, overgrown, humid | Bioluminescent flowers as light sources, slow drifting particles, exotica |
| The ballroom | Euphoria, flow | Long vertical shafts, chandeliers to swing from, the fastest movement in the game, four-on-the-floor |
| The baths | Dissolution, drifting | Swimming, steam, muffled sound, nothing quite in focus |
| After hours | Comedown, loneliness | Empty back rooms, flickering cold light, slowed-down echoes of earlier music |
| Dawn | Clarity, or emptiness | Natural daylight for the first time, silence, no enemies |

The biomes are working names.
The order is the intended emotional arc, not a fixed route;
the world is open, so a player may see the ballroom before the feast.

## The central loop of feeling

This is the mechanic that carries the theme, so it gets its own section.

You collect Nectar, a golden, sweet, abstract resource,
from enemies, hidden places and the leftovers of the party.
You spend it on one thing: a High.
Healing is apart from it, in a few Cordials refilled at each rest,
so reaching for Nectar is always a choice to indulge, never a need.

During a High the world saturates, the music opens up with every stem playing,
you move a little faster and hit a little harder.
When it ends, the Comedown follows:
colour drains, the music thins and sinks behind a low-pass filter,
and you are briefly slower and more fragile.

Tolerance builds with each High, so the next one is shorter.
Resting at a lounge, the save point, lowers tolerance again but never to zero.

Separately, the world fades as you progress.
Each major ability you gain takes a little colour out of the world's surfaces.
Light never fades.
By the late game the palace is grey stone and vivid lamps,
and the High is the only way to see the colour you remember.
So the game makes the High more tempting exactly as it makes it less effective.

This was the riskiest idea in the design, since a grey late game can feel dull rather than haunting.
The ballroom prototype showed it landing, so it stays;
[progression](progression.md) sets how much colour each ability takes.

## Death

Dying is not a disaster; nothing is.
You black out and wake at the last lounge with a hangover,
which is a Comedown that lasts until you rest or indulge.
You lose no currency and no progress.
The penalty is a feeling, not a number.

## Abilities

Abilities gate the map in the usual metroidvania way.
Each one is a movement verb first and a theme second.

| Ability | Verb | What it opens | Theme |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lunge | Air and ground dash | Wide gaps, dash-through curtains | Impatience |
| Swoon | Double jump with a slow, floating apex | High ledges | Lightheadedness |
| Cling | Wall cling and wall jump | Vertical shafts | Holding on |
| Lure | Grapple, but only to light sources | Chandeliers, lanterns, glowing flowers | Moth to the flame |
| Plunge | Fast downward slam that breaks floors | Cracked floors, deep shafts | Diving in |
| Submerge | Swim and dive in nectar pools | The baths, flooded cellars | Drowning in it |
| Drift | Glide on rising warm air | Long horizontal crossings over the gardens | Letting go |
| Numb | Pass through glass and thorn hazards unharmed | The last areas | Feeling nothing |

Lure is the signature ability.
It ties traversal to light, so the art rule and the movement rule become the same rule:
you can only go where something glows.

Numb comes last on purpose.
It makes you invulnerable to the world's sharp edges,
and it takes the most colour of all.

## Combat

Combat is heavy and committed; [combat](combat.md) has the details.
There are three weapons, all taken from the party:
a sabre for opening champagne, a champagne bottle the size of a person swung like a maul,
and a burning candelabrum used as a polearm.
A parry, the Toast, rings like two glasses meeting.

Enemies are other guests, lost in their own loops:
a dancer who cannot stop, a diner who keeps eating, a sleeper who lashes out when woken.
They are pitiful as much as dangerous.

## Characters and story

The story lives in the rooms, in a few lines of dialogue, and in what the guests are doing.
There is no narrator and no lore dump.

The guests each embody one answer to the question.
One wants only the absence of pain and has stopped moving.
One wants the most intense pleasure and is burning out.
One insists the party means something and is the loneliest of all.
The Host, who threw the party and never stopped hosting it, sits at the centre of the palace.

The endings do not rank the answers.
You can find the Host and keep the party going, starting the night again.
Or you can walk out of the palace into the morning;
a short, silent walk in daylight with nothing left to do.

## Inspirations

These set the tone; we take mood from them, never plot, characters or images.

Poe's *The Masque of the Red Death*:
a party sealed away from a dying world, rooms each in its own colour, the last one black.
The Cyrenaic philosophers, for whom the pleasure of the present moment was the only good.
Khayyam's *Rubaiyat* and *Ecclesiastes*: eat, drink, for it is all vapour.
Fellini's *La Dolce Vita* and the feast of Trimalchio in the *Satyricon*.
Weimar cabaret; Gatsby's parties seen from the empty lawn the morning after.

## Tone guardrails

We explore emptiness without glamorising self-destruction.
The game never presents death or self-harm as the answer;
death in the game is a blackout and a hangover, nothing more.

Nectar stays abstract: sweet, golden and glowing, never a named drink or drug.
This keeps the metaphor open to the player's own reading,
and a literal drug or alcohol mechanic would raise the age rating on every storefront.
